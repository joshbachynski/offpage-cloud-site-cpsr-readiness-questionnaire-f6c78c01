CPSR readiness questionnaire: a practical way to assess procurement compliance readiness

CPSR readiness questionnaire is a structured self-assessment used to identify gaps in purchasing, supplier oversight, recordkeeping, and internal controls before a Contractor Purchasing System Review. It helps organizations see where their processes are documented, consistent, and defensible. Used early, it can reduce surprises during a formal review.

If you are preparing for a purchasing system review, the CPSR readiness questionnaire can help you organize evidence and spot weaknesses before an auditor does. The goal is not just to “pass” an assessment, but to understand whether your purchasing process is repeatable, approved, and supported by records. A good questionnaire turns a broad compliance question into a practical checklist of controls, documents, and responsibilities.

  • Confirms whether policies match day-to-day purchasing practice
  • Identifies missing approvals, logs, and training records
  • Highlights supplier file, competition, and price analysis gaps
  • Shows whether buyers can demonstrate consistent decision-making

For many contractors, readiness issues appear in small but important details: a file without evidence of competition, a purchase order missing a signature, or a procedure that exists in writing but is not followed in practice. A well-designed questionnaire helps teams catch those problems while there is still time to correct them. It also creates a common language for procurement, contracts, finance, and quality teams that may each hold part of the evidence needed for a review.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: what it evaluates in a purchasing system

A CPSR readiness questionnaire evaluates whether your purchasing system is controlled, documented, and supported by evidence. It typically looks at policy compliance, delegation of authority, supplier selection, file completeness, and the ability to track purchasing actions from request to award.

The most useful questionnaires focus on both process and proof. They ask not only whether a procedure exists, but also whether employees can show current examples that demonstrate the procedure is being followed. That distinction matters because a strong written policy with weak execution can still create risk during a review.

Common evaluation areas include purchase request approval, competition or sole-source justification, price analysis, flowdown of required terms, supplier responsibility checks, and maintenance of complete purchasing records. Many organizations also review segregation of duties, ethics training, and escalation paths for exceptions. When these items are examined together, the questionnaire can reveal whether the purchasing system is mature enough for formal scrutiny.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: core sections to include in the assessment

A strong questionnaire should cover the major controls that support compliant purchasing. Each section should ask for evidence, a responsible owner, and a clear yes, no, or not applicable answer where possible.

Start with policy and procedure questions. Ask whether current purchasing policies are approved, dated, distributed to the right staff, and aligned with actual workflows. Include questions about whether revisions are controlled and whether employees can locate the latest version quickly.

Next, assess delegation and authority. The questionnaire should confirm who may approve requisitions, issue purchase orders, negotiate terms, and sign exceptions. It should also ask whether those authorities are documented and whether temporary authority changes are tracked.

Then focus on supplier management and file integrity. Good questions probe vendor onboarding, due diligence, performance tracking, and the presence of required documents in each file. A thorough assessment should also check whether procurement personnel retain enough evidence to explain why a supplier was selected and why the price was considered fair and reasonable.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: evidence items that strengthen each response

Evidence makes the questionnaire useful. Without documents, screenshots, or sample files, answers may sound correct but remain unverified.

Useful evidence items often include current policies, sample purchase files, approval logs, training records, supplier lists, price analyses, source selection notes, and corrective action records. If your organization uses an ERP or procurement system, screenshots or reports may also help show how approvals and controls work in practice. The stronger the evidence, the easier it is to identify whether a control is actually operating.

Many teams find it helpful to attach one or two representative examples to each major question. That approach keeps the questionnaire from becoming an abstract survey and turns it into a working file review tool. It also makes follow-up easier when a control needs clarification or remediation.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: how to use it before a formal review

Use the questionnaire as an internal diagnostic tool, not a one-time form. Review the results, assign owners, set deadlines, and recheck the same areas after fixes are made.
  1. Distribute the questionnaire to procurement, contracts, finance, and compliance stakeholders.
  2. Collect current policies, sample files, and process evidence for each answer.
  3. Mark gaps by severity, such as missing documentation, inconsistent practice, or unclear ownership.
  4. Assign corrective actions and target dates for each gap.
  5. Retest a small sample of files after remediation to confirm the fix is working.

This process works best when it is collaborative. Procurement may know the actual workflow, contracts may understand clause requirements, and finance may hold payment or approval records that complete the story. When those groups review the questionnaire together, the organization is more likely to find root causes instead of only correcting surface-level symptoms.

Timing matters as well. A questionnaire completed too close to a review leaves little room to gather evidence or retrain staff. Starting early gives teams time to close documentation gaps, update procedures, and verify that the process still works after changes are made. In practice, the most useful questionnaires are revisited regularly rather than saved for a single audit cycle.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: common red flags it can uncover

Frequent red flags include incomplete purchase files, unclear approval authority, weak justification for supplier selection, and procedures that do not match actual behavior. These issues often point to control design problems or training gaps.

One common warning sign is inconsistent documentation. If one buyer keeps detailed records while another keeps only a final purchase order, the organization may not be able to show a consistent control environment. Another red flag is when teams know the right answer informally but cannot point to a policy, form, or system record that proves it.

Other issues include outdated procedures, missing contract flowdowns, unexplained exceptions, and weak retention practices. A questionnaire can also uncover dependency on a single employee who understands the process but has not documented it. That kind of knowledge concentration creates risk if the person is unavailable when a review begins.

CPSR readiness questionnaire: how to turn results into action

The most valuable result is a clear action plan. Each gap should have an owner, a due date, and a verification step so the organization can demonstrate improvement.

After scoring or reviewing responses, group findings into themes such as training, documentation, supplier control, approval workflow, and record retention. That structure helps leaders prioritize the highest-risk problems first. It also prevents the team from treating every issue as equally urgent when some gaps are minor and others affect system credibility.

Track remediation in a simple register that lists the issue, impact, root cause, action taken, and evidence of closure. When possible, validate fixes with sample files or process walkthroughs. If the organization can show that corrective action changed the way work is actually performed, the questionnaire becomes more than a checklist—it becomes part of a continuous compliance process.

For companies seeking a practical, repeatable way to prepare for review, a well-built CPSR readiness questionnaire is one of the most useful tools available. It helps teams understand where they stand, what evidence they need, and which controls deserve attention first. Most importantly, it turns compliance preparation into a manageable, structured exercise instead of a last-minute scramble.
