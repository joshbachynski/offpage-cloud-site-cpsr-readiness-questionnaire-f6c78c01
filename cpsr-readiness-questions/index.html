CPSR readiness questions for a reliable pre-review check

A CPSR readiness questionnaire is a structured set of questions used to check whether a business is prepared for a Contractor Purchasing System Review. It helps identify gaps in purchasing controls, documentation, ethics, and subcontract management before an audit or formal review. The goal is to surface weak points early so the organization can correct them with less risk and less disruption.

Before a formal review, the most useful approach is to ask questions that reveal how purchasing actually works, not just how the written procedure is supposed to work. A strong readiness check looks at policy, approval authority, competition, supplier selection, file documentation, and follow-through after award. If those areas are consistent, traceable, and supported by records, the organization is usually in a much better position to face a CPSR.

  • Does the purchasing system have current written procedures?
  • Are buyer authorities and approval limits clearly defined?
  • Do purchase files show adequate competition or a valid source selection rationale?
  • Are subcontract and flowdown requirements handled consistently?
  • Can the team produce complete records quickly when asked?

CPSR readiness questions that test whether your procedures are current and usable

The first readiness check is whether the company can prove its purchasing process is documented, current, and actually followed. Reviewers expect procedures that match day-to-day practice and that are controlled well enough to show who approved them and when they changed.

Ask whether all major purchasing activities are covered in policy, including requisition review, solicitation, evaluation, award, modifications, and file retention. It is not enough to have a manual on paper if buyers rely on unwritten habits or email shortcuts that are never captured in the file. A readiness question should therefore ask: can a new buyer read the procedure and repeat the process without extra coaching?

Another useful question is whether the procedure addresses both routine buys and exceptions. Many systems are strongest in normal cases but weaker when there is urgency, a sole source justification, a protest risk, or a subcontract that needs special clauses. If those exceptions are handled inconsistently, the review may find that control depends on individual judgment rather than a repeatable process.

CPSR readiness questions that verify buyer authority and approval controls

A purchasing system is ready only when approval authority is clear, documented, and respected in practice. The review should be able to follow who can commit the company, who can approve awards, and who reviews higher-risk or higher-dollar actions.

One of the most important questions is whether every buyer knows the limit of their authority. If a buyer can negotiate terms but cannot award without approval, the file should show that approval. If authority shifts by contract type, dollar value, or customer requirement, the readiness questionnaire should test whether those thresholds are understood and consistently applied.

You should also ask whether delegation letters, signature controls, or system permissions are current. Weak approval control often appears as missing signatures, approvals made after award, or files that do not show who performed a review. These are not just administrative issues; they can signal that the system is not enforcing the intended controls.

CPSR readiness questions for supplier selection, competition, and price reasonableness

Competitive sourcing and price analysis are central indicators of purchasing system strength. Readiness questions should show whether the company can justify why a supplier was selected and why the price was considered fair and reasonable.

Ask whether the team can explain how competition is obtained for common buys and what happens when competition is limited. The file should usually show quotations, bids, or another documented market check, along with the rationale for the final selection. Where competition is not available, the record should show why and what alternate analysis supported the decision.

Price reasonableness is another area where readiness questions can be very revealing. The organization should know when it uses price comparisons, prior purchases, catalog evidence, labor rate checks, independent estimates, or other analysis methods. A helpful question is whether the method used is appropriate to the buy and whether the supporting data is kept in the file in a way a reviewer can follow.

It is also smart to ask whether the supplier evaluation process includes quality, delivery, risk, and past performance where relevant. A low price does not automatically create a strong procurement decision. If the documentation shows only the cheapest quote without any explanation of suitability, the system may appear undercontrolled even if the purchase eventually works out.

CPSR readiness questions about subcontracting, flowdowns, and file completeness

Subcontract files are ready when they show the business reason for the award, the applicable clauses or flowdowns, and the records needed to support later review. Missing attachments and incomplete files are common causes of avoidable findings.

Ask whether the company has a consistent method for determining which clauses, terms, and customer requirements must flow down to suppliers. If different buyers rely on memory or copy old templates without checking applicability, the resulting subcontract package may be incomplete. A good readiness review tests whether clause selection is tied to contract type, order value, work scope, and customer flowdown obligations.

File completeness matters just as much as correct clause selection. The file should generally show the request, solicitation or RFQ, received quotes, evaluation notes, award approval, and any post-award changes. If key documents live in email threads, shared drives, or individual inboxes, the organization may struggle to reconstruct the decision path during a review.

To make readiness practical, ask whether the file can be assembled quickly and whether someone unfamiliar with the purchase could understand it. If the answer is no, the issue is usually not a single missing paper but a broader control problem. That is exactly the kind of weakness a CPSR is designed to uncover.

CPSR readiness questions that reveal training, monitoring, and corrective action gaps

Training and monitoring are ready when employees know the process, management checks compliance, and problems are corrected before they recur. A system without ongoing oversight may look fine until someone asks for evidence.

One of the most practical readiness questions is whether buyers, supervisors, and reviewers receive training on the current procedures. Training should not be limited to onboarding; it should also cover updates, common errors, and lessons learned from internal reviews. If the team cannot explain why a control exists, it may be easy to bypass under schedule pressure.

It is equally important to ask how the company monitors purchasing performance. Internal audits, file reviews, sampling, and management oversight can reveal patterns such as missing support, late approvals, or inconsistent use of competition. A readiness questionnaire should identify whether findings are tracked, assigned, and closed with documented corrective action.

For a truly useful CPSR readiness questionnaire, the questions should lead to evidence, not opinions. Each answer should point to a file, report, approval trail, or procedure that can be shown to a reviewer. When the business can answer those questions confidently and support each answer with records, it is much closer to being audit ready and less likely to be surprised by avoidable findings.

Strong readiness is not about having perfect paperwork everywhere. It is about being able to show a repeatable purchasing process, documented decisions, and timely control over exceptions.
